Where Does Jagged Competence Come From? investigates why networks combine low average error with failures on particular inputs, in a task constructed from two known layers. Layer A computes five per-slot sums from records; layer B combines their slot-1 category with a hidden on/off mode carried from earlier boards to determine the probabilities of the next board's category. The current sums alone do not determine that law: B strictly extends A in the sense of Six Birds Theory. Because both layers are known exactly, the study asks whether training on the final prediction learns this decomposition, and measures separately whether the lower computation is recoverable, computed, delivered to the prediction and preserved, and whether prediction is reliable.

In r8, records-to-prediction networks (three seeds) reach a natural KL below 3×10−4 bits, while their maximum law TV on constructed histories is about 0.25. Using a census of all 24,435 boards, constructed history panels, calibrated probes and matched experiments, the paper finds that these achievements come apart. Connecting a learned sums output to the prediction cuts categorical misreads 1.3–17 fold; the same exact sums give 0–2 misreads as one-hot inputs and 10–317 as numerical inputs; one prediction update makes an exact sums computation inexact in all three tested optimizer conditions, with very different severity, while blocking the prediction gradient preserves it; target noise contributes in a matched comparison and history coverage in a historical one; and some failures remain unexplained even with exact sums supplied. Whether the same sources operate in other systems is posed as a test to run.
